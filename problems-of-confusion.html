Introduction

Clear communication is an important part of leadership, learning, and technical work. A message can be organized and accurate, but it is only effective when the audience understands it as intended. In AI and software projects, unclear requirements or unexplained technical language can create different interpretations, slow progress, and lead to unnecessary rework.

This artifact brings together two pieces of my course work. Assignment 4.1, “Problems of Confusion,” helped me reflect on my communication style and a software project where unclear documentation caused team members to implement requirements differently. Activity 3.4 gave me an opportunity to practice explaining a technical AI concept by using Face ID as a familiar example of how a trained neural network is applied.

Together, these activities show that communication is not simply about providing information. It requires understanding the audience, choosing an appropriate explanation, documenting decisions, and confirming that everyone has the same understanding.

Personal Value Statement

I value communication that is clear, organized, respectful, and appropriate for the audience. My goal is not only to deliver information, but to create shared understanding so that people know the purpose, expectations, and next steps.

I would describe my communication style as organized and straightforward. I prefer to explain the goal first and then provide the details. At work, I summarize meetings, document action items, and ask questions whenever requirements are unclear. I also believe that strong communication includes listening, inviting questions, and being willing to explain an idea in a different way when the first explanation does not connect.

This value is especially important in AI and software engineering. Technical decisions affect developers, product teams, business stakeholders, and users who may have different levels of technical knowledge. I want my communication to make complex work understandable without removing the details people need to make informed decisions.

Strategies to Fulfill This Value

Document Decisions and Confirm Alignment

During a software project involving updates to an evaluation platform, feature requirements were discussed in meetings but not every decision was documented clearly. Team members interpreted the requirements differently and implemented parts of the feature in different ways. We then spent additional time reviewing the requirements, correcting the differences, and updating the implementation.

That experience showed me that clear documentation saves time and prevents avoidable rework. To help a team remain aligned, I use the following strategies:

  • Document important decisions after meetings.
  • Identify owners, action items, and expected outcomes.
  • Ask follow-up questions when a requirement could have more than one interpretation.
  • Use examples or acceptance criteria to make expectations concrete.
  • Invite teammates to ask questions or restate the plan before implementation begins.

Adapt the Explanation to the Audience

Activity 3.4 helped me practice this strategy with an AI topic. I used Face ID because it is familiar to many people. I explained that the neural network has already learned patterns from many face images during training. When a person sets up Face ID, the phone captures information about that individual’s face and uses the trained system to recognize the user later.

I introduced the process in small steps instead of using many technical terms at once. My goal was to help someone without a machine-learning background understand the difference between training a model and using a trained model in a real application. The peer feedback I received confirmed that the familiar example made the concept approachable and also encouraged me to make the training-versus-setup distinction even clearer.

Check for Understanding

Whether I am explaining an AI concept or clarifying software requirements, I should not assume that everyone interpreted the message in the same way. I can ask for questions, summarize the decision, and confirm the next steps before the team begins implementation.

Key Learnings
  • Providing information is not the same as creating shared understanding.
  • Familiar examples can make complex AI concepts easier to understand.
  • Technical explanations should match the audience’s background and needs.
  • Written decisions and action items give a team one shared reference.
  • Questions and confirmation can identify different interpretations before they become rework.
  • Clear communication is an important AI competency because it connects technical systems with people, decisions, and outcomes.

These activities also reinforced the course’s biblical emphasis on speaking with wisdom, promoting understanding, and working together in unity rather than allowing confusion to create unnecessary conflict.
