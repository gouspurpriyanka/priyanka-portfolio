Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning

Priyanka Gouspur · AIML-500: Machine Learning Fundamentals · Indiana Wesleyan University · July 14, 2026

Back to Artifacts

Introduction

Machine learning and deep learning are related, but they are not always used for the same types of problems. Deep learning may seem like the better option because it is more advanced, but that does not mean it is the right choice in every situation. The decision depends on the type of data, the goal of the model, and how the results will be used.

Key Differences

Area Machine Learning Deep Learning
Approach Uses algorithms such as decision trees, regression, support vector machines, and clustering. Uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn increasingly complex representations.
Data Can perform well with smaller, structured datasets. Usually benefits from large datasets, especially for complex tasks.
Features Often relies on people to identify and prepare useful input features. Can learn relevant features directly from raw or minimally processed data.
Computing Frequently trains on standard computing resources. Often requires more processing power, memory, and specialized accelerators.
Interpretability Many models are easier to inspect and explain. Complex networks can be difficult to interpret without additional techniques.
Training Time Often faster to train and iterate on. Training can take substantially longer depending on model and dataset size.

The comparison above provides a general framework. The following two real-world cases show why the correct choice depends on the form of the data, the operational goal, and the need for explainable results.

Example 1

Customer Churn Prediction

Real-World Application

Customer churn prediction is commonly used by telecommunications companies. These companies want to identify customers who may cancel their service so they can take action before that happens. A churn model may use customer information such as contract type, monthly charges, length of service, payment method, service subscriptions, and support history. The model produces a risk score that shows how likely a customer is to leave. A retention team can then contact the customer, offer a discount, recommend a different plan, or address a service issue.

One telecom churn model used customer records and social-network information to identify subscribers who were likely to leave. The model achieved an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 93.3% and was used to support customer-retention efforts.

Why Traditional Machine Learning Fits

Traditional machine learning works well for churn prediction because the data is structured. Customer information is usually stored in tables, with each column representing a clear feature such as tenure, contract type, monthly cost, or support calls. These features are already understood by the company. The model does not need to discover what they mean. Analysts and business teams already know that price, contract length, customer-service issues, and service quality may affect whether someone stays or leaves.

Simpler models often perform well on this type of data. Logistic regression, random forests, support vector machines, and XGBoost can all identify relationships between customer characteristics and churn. In some studies, logistic regression has performed almost as well as more complex models.

Interpretability is also important. A company does not only need to know that a customer may leave; it also needs to understand why. A customer who is unhappy with the price may need a discount, while a customer with repeated technical problems may need better support. Traditional models make it easier to see which features influenced the prediction, helping the company decide what action to take.

Why Deep Learning May Be Unnecessary

Deep learning can also be used for churn prediction and may improve accuracy in some cases. However, it may not be necessary for a problem that already has structured data and clear features. Deep-learning models often require more data, computing power, and training time. They can also be harder to explain.

A deep-learning model may predict that a customer is likely to leave without clearly showing the business team which factors caused that result. This is a problem because the company needs more than a risk score—it needs information that can lead to an action. If a model is slightly more accurate but much harder to understand, the extra accuracy may provide little practical value. Traditional machine learning is therefore often a better fit because it provides useful predictions while helping the company understand the main causes of churn.

Example 2

Autonomous Driving

Real-World Application

Autonomous driving is an example where deep learning is needed. Companies such as Waymo use cameras, radar, and LiDAR sensors to understand what is happening around a vehicle. The system must recognize cars, pedestrians, cyclists, traffic lights, road signs, lane markings, and other objects. It must also track movement and make decisions while the vehicle is moving.

The data comes from images and sensor readings rather than simple rows and columns. A self-driving vehicle must process this information quickly because even a small delay can affect safety.

Why Deep Learning Fits

Deep learning works well for autonomous driving because it can process large amounts of unstructured data. Images contain thousands or millions of pixels, and LiDAR systems produce large point clouds that represent the vehicle’s surroundings. The important features are not already listed in a table. The system has to learn which shapes, edges, colors, movements, and patterns are related to cars, people, signs, and other objects.

Convolutional neural networks are useful because they can learn visual features directly from images. Early layers may identify edges and shapes, while later layers may recognize complete objects. Deep learning is also useful because real-world driving conditions change constantly. A pedestrian may appear at night, in the rain, behind another vehicle, or from a different angle. It would be difficult for a person to create a fixed set of rules for every possible situation. A model can instead learn from many examples and become better at recognizing objects under different conditions. Companies working on autonomous vehicles also have access to the large datasets and computing resources needed to train these models.

Why Traditional Machine Learning Falls Short

Traditional machine learning would require engineers to select the important features before training the model. This may work when the data is structured, but it is not practical for raw camera images and LiDAR data. Engineers would have to manually define which image features represent a pedestrian, vehicle, bicycle, or traffic sign. Those features would also need to work across different weather conditions, lighting, angles, distances, and levels of visibility.

It is not realistic to create rules for every possible driving situation. A traditional model would have difficulty using raw image pixels because it would not automatically learn the patterns needed to identify objects. Traditional machine learning is also not designed to handle the same volume and complexity of visual and sensor data. Autonomous driving requires the system to recognize objects and react in real time. Deep learning is more suitable because it can learn the necessary features directly from the data.

Conclusion

Customer churn prediction and autonomous driving show why the choice between machine learning and deep learning depends on the problem. Customer churn prediction uses structured data with known features, and the business needs to understand why a customer may leave. Traditional machine learning is a good choice because it can provide accurate results and explanations that the company can use.

Autonomous driving uses images and sensor data. The system must learn patterns that cannot be manually defined and must recognize objects under many different conditions. Deep learning is better suited for this task because it can learn features directly from large amounts of data.
